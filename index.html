What your results mean

    How to read your speed test results

    Your test produces three numbers, and each one describes a different part of your connection. Ping measures responsiveness — how long a tiny signal takes to travel to a server and back. Download speed measures how fast data flows to your device, which is what streaming, browsing and downloading files depend on. Upload speed measures how fast data flows from your device, which matters for video calls, cloud backups and sending large files. Our guide to download vs upload speed explains why the two numbers are rarely equal, and why your plan almost certainly advertises the bigger one.

    Wondering whether your numbers are actually any good? That depends entirely on what you do online. A single person streaming HD video needs far less than a household of five with two people on video calls and a teenager gaming. We break the numbers down activity by activity in our guide to what a good internet speed is — including the speeds Netflix, YouTube, Zoom and major games really use, which are often lower than people expect.

    Gamers should pay special attention to the first number. Raw bandwidth barely matters for online play; what decides whether a game feels sharp or laggy is ping and latency, plus its less famous cousins, jitter and packet loss. If your ping is high even though your download speed looks fine, that article explains why — and what actually fixes it.

    Why your result might look disappointing

    Your plan speed is a maximum, not a promise

    Internet plans are sold as "up to" speeds measured under ideal conditions on a wired connection. Real life includes Wi-Fi walls, neighbours on the same channel, older routers and peak-hour congestion. If your result is far below what you pay for even on a wired test, our checklist of 12 fixes for slow internet walks through every likely cause, from router placement to the one background app quietly eating your bandwidth.

    Wi-Fi is usually the bottleneck

    Most homes never see their full plan speed over Wi-Fi, especially a room or two away from the router. A cable removes interference, distance and contention from the equation entirely. Our comparison of Wi-Fi vs Ethernet shows the real-world speed and latency differences, and helps you decide which devices are worth wiring up.

    Quick reference: speed by activity

    Everyday browsing, email and social media need only about 3–5 Mbps, and even a ping under 100 ms feels fine for them. HD video streaming uses 5–8 Mbps per stream, while a 4K stream needs 15–25 Mbps — the biggest single demand in most homes. An HD video call uses roughly 3–4 Mbps in each direction and feels best with ping under 80 ms. Online gaming itself uses just 3–6 Mbps, but it is the most ping-sensitive activity of all: under 50 ms is where it feels sharp. Broadcasting your own live stream is the hungriest job here, needing 10–25 Mbps of upload. And large downloads simply go faster the more download speed you have — there is no "enough," only how long you are willing to wait.

    These are per-activity figures. Add up everything your household does at the same time, then compare the total with the good internet speed your home actually needs.
